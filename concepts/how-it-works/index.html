How it works

Each worker has two moving parts: one event loop that decides what runs next, and a pool of goroutines that run your function. A job travels from Add, through the queue and the event loop, to a pool goroutine. The diagrams below loop, and each one steps through a single case.

The event loop

Section titled “The event loop”

Every worker runs one event loop, a single goroutine. It sleeps until something signals it: Add putting a job in the queue, or a pool goroutine finishing work. On each signal it hands jobs to the pool while the queue has work and a slot is free, then goes back to sleep.

A slot is free while the number of busy pool goroutines is below the worker’s concurrency. If no slot is free, the loop waits for a goroutine to come back.

The worker pool

Section titled “The worker pool”

Every Add, and every job in an AddAll, tries to wake the event loop. The signal channel holds only one pending signal, so the extra signals are dropped. That is enough: one wake-up lets the loop keep dispatching back to back while the queue has work and a slot is free. A signal that arrives while another is pending costs nothing.

The concurrency limit is an upper bound, not a pool size. The pool starts with one idle goroutine and spawns more while another one would drain the backlog faster than it costs. When a goroutine finishes, it stays in the pool if more jobs are waiting than idle goroutines are ready to run them. Otherwise it exits, and the pool shrinks back to one idle goroutine. The diagrams label the goroutines Worker 1, Worker 2 and so on. WithIdleWorkerExpiryDuration and WithMinIdleWorkerRatio change how many idle goroutines stay around. Concurrency covers both.

Below, six jobs are added to a worker with concurrency 4. Four go out back to back, the other two wait in the queue, and the pool shrinks as it drains.

Topics

Section titled “Topics”

A Topic feeds the same event loop as a queue, but from a shared log instead of a queue of jobs. Publish appends the payload to the log once, then signals the event loop of every subscribed worker, the same one-slot signal Add sends. Each subscription reads the log through its own cursor, so each worker’s loop makes its own job from the payload and hands it to its own pool. The workers run independently: a slow or paused one does not hold up Publish or the others.

A payload stays in the log until every subscription has read it. Below, two workers subscribe to one topic, and the slower one keeps a chunk of the log alive until its cursor moves past it.

Batched dispatch

Section titled “Batched dispatch”

A hand-off costs the event loop the same whether a job takes a nanosecond or a minute. With many short jobs, the loop, not the goroutines, becomes the bottleneck. To avoid that, a goroutine can take several jobs at once, a batch, and run them one after another.

The batch size is the smallest of three numbers:

  • an even split of the waiting jobs across the workers the limit allows;
  • enough jobs to keep hand-offs about 32 microseconds apart, sized from the last batch that finished (one job until a job has been timed, unless concurrency is 1);

With enough jobs waiting, that works out like this:

Setup Jobs per hand-off
10 goroutines, 3 s jobs 1, so jobs start in queue order
24 goroutines, 1 ms jobs 1
3,000 goroutines, 10 ms jobs 10
10,000 or more goroutines, 10 ms jobs 32
Concurrency 1, any jobs up to 32, because one goroutine runs jobs in order anyway

The diagram shows a worker that has already timed its jobs, with 8 cheap jobs and concurrency 2. The first goroutine takes 4 jobs, a second goroutine is spawned for 2, and the last 2 jobs go out one at a time as goroutines free up. A fresh worker hands out one job at a time until its first batch finishes.

What batching means for your code

Section titled “What batching means for your code”
  • Jobs waiting in a batch are still Queued. The worker’s NumPending counts them and NumProcessing does not. You can still close them.
  • On Stop or Pause, each goroutine finishes its running job and parks the rest of its batch. NumPending counts the parked jobs, but the queue’s Len() does not. They run first, in order, after Resume or Restart, and Purge closes them. See the lifecycle.
  • Capacity counts only jobs still in the queue. A bounded queue can have up to 32 more jobs per goroutine pending, held in batches.

Not every source batches:

Source Batches? Why
In-memory queue, topic Yes
Priority queue No A higher-priority job added later must not wait behind a batch.
Persistent or distributed queue No A worker must not hold unacknowledged messages that other consumers could take.
Worker bound to several queues No The strategy picks a queue for every single job.
